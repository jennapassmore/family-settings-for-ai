AI safeguard prototype

Who gets to read a teen's AI chat?

Teens tell AI things they'd never tell their parents.
That's the point, and the problem.

At Discord, I led the redesign of Family Center, which lets teens share what they're doing without sharing what they're saying. AI makes that line harder to hold: the teen isn't messaging a friend, they're confiding in the product. I built this prototype with Claude Code to figure out how much a parent should actually see.

Looking away, looking out, or looking over their shoulder?

Every setting trades safety against trust. The trick is giving up as little of either as possible.

      Keep parents in the loop, not in the chat

      The approach that held up across every scenario gives the parent enough context to know their teen is safe, and nothing more:

      • Activity, not messages. The parent sees when and how long the AI was used.
      • A nudge, not a transcript. When something looks unsafe, the parent is prompted to check in, without the details.
      • No surprises. The teen sees exactly what the parent sees, and can add context before a nudge is sent.
      • Agreed, not imposed. The family chooses the settings together.

      These are hypotheses, not findings. The next step is testing them with real families.

      The thinking behind it

      Still here? Here's where I show my work.

      The difference

      On a platform, a parent can see who their teen talks to. With AI, the teen talks to the product.

      Family controls on social platforms are built around contacts and activity: who a teen is talking to, and where they spend time. With an AI, the "contact" is the product itself, and the conversation is often private, personal and exploratory. Showing a parent the chat isn't showing them a profile. It's closer to reading a diary.

      The challenge

      More oversight can easily become surveillance

      When I redesigned Family Center, new regulations required giving parents significantly more visibility and control. The product was moving from monitoring to managing, and the risk was that more parental oversight would turn into surveillance. AI brings the same tension, with higher stakes.

      The stakes

      Too little, and a real risk is missed. Too much, and the teen leaves.

      In the prototype, hands-off settings miss the safety moment entirely. Watching everything catches it, but exposes a teen asking about their identity, and pushes them toward apps nobody can see. Even when the teen knows they're being watched, heavy monitoring carries that workaround risk. Transparency alone doesn't fix collecting too much.

      The approach

      Carry over what worked: the teen sees what the parent sees

      In Family Center, messages stayed private, teens could see exactly what their parents could see, and sensitive moments created a chance to talk instead of automatically exposing the details. The AI version follows the same principles: activity, not messages; a nudge, not a transcript; and settings the family agrees on together.
